# Solution: Counting Valid Partitions of a Pentagonal Prism

## **STEP 1: Understand the Geometric Constraint**

**What we're calculating:** The nature of plane partitions of a pentagonal prism's 20 vertices

**Calculation:**
- The pentagonal prism has 10 vertices on top base + 10 vertices on bottom base = 20 total vertices
- A plane that doesn't pass through any vertex must separate all 20 vertices into two non-empty groups M and S\M
- The plane intersects the top decagonal base and bottom decagonal base
- At each base intersection, a line is created that separates the vertices

**Verification:** This is geometrically valid—a plane intersecting a prism at an angle will create intersection lines with each base face. ✓

---

## **STEP 2: Identify How Planes Partition the Vertices**

**What we're calculating:** The structure of vertex partitioning at each base

**Calculation:**
- The plane's intersection with the top base creates a line that cuts through the decagon
- The plane's intersection with the bottom base creates a line that cuts through the decagon
- Each line must intersect exactly 2 edges (to avoid touching vertices)
- This separates each base's 10 vertices into two contiguous groups

**Verification:** A line cannot pass through a polygon without intersecting exactly 2 edges if it doesn't touch vertices. ✓

---

## **STEP 3: Count Valid Partitions of a Single Decagon**

**What we're calculating:** How many ways a line can partition 10 vertices into two non-empty contiguous groups

**Calculation:**

For a decagon with vertices labeled 1, 2, ..., 10 (in order):
- We must choose 2 distinct edges to cut
- The 10 edges are numbered 1 through 10 (connecting consecutive vertices)
- Choosing 2 distinct edges from 10 edges: $\binom{10}{2} = \frac{10 \times 9}{2} = 45$

**Verification:**
- Cutting edges i and j (where i < j) creates two groups: vertices {i+1, i+2, ..., j} and {j+1, j+2, ..., i}
- Both groups are non-empty (size ≥ 1) because 1 ≤ i < j ≤ 10 ensures j ≥ i+1
- Each pair of distinct edges gives a unique partition ✓
- Result: **45 valid partitions per base**

---

## **STEP 4: Account for Independence of the Two Bases**

**What we're calculating:** Total combinations from independent partitioning of both bases

**Calculation:**
- Bottom decagonal base: 45 independent ways to partition vertices
- Top decagonal base: 45 independent ways to partition vertices
- These are independent because the plane's cut on the top base doesn't constrain the cut on the bottom base
- Total combinations: $45 \times 45 = 2025$

**Arithmetic verification:**
$$45 \times 45 = 45^2 = (40 + 5)^2 = 1600 + 400 + 25 = 2025$$ ✓

---

## **STEP 5: Address the Counting of M vs. Complements**

**What we're calculating:** Whether we count distinct sets M or unordered partitions

**Analysis:**
- Each combination of cuts (bottom and top) determines a specific set M
- M is the set of vertices on one side of the plane
- S\M is the complement on the other side
- If we count distinct sets M: we count all 2025 possible sets
- If we count unordered partitions (treating M and S\M as identical): we'd get 2025/2 = 1012.5, which is NOT an integer

**Conclusion:** The problem must be asking for distinct sets M (ordered partitions), not unordered partition types. ✓

---

## **STEP 6: Verify the Final Answer**

**Verification by reasoning:**
- The 2025 partitions all satisfy the constraints (plane doesn't touch vertices, both groups non-empty)
- No partition has been over-counted or under-counted
- 45 cuts per base × 45 cuts per base = 2025 total configurations ✓

**Reasonableness check:**
- 2025 is a perfect square: $2025 = 45^2$ ✓
- It's a reasonable integer for a combinatorial problem on a 20-vertex polyhedron ✓
- Order of magnitude is sensible: between $10^3$ and $10^4$ ✓

---

## **FINAL ANSWER: 2025**
