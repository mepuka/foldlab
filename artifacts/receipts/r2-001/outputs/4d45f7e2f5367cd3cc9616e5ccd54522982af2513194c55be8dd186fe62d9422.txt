# Solution to Competition Math Problem

## Step 1: Set up inclusion-exclusion equations

**What we're calculating:** Express the relationship between total full moon moments and those with multiple simultaneous full moons.

**Calculation:**
Let:
- $A$ = moments when Io has a full moon
- $B$ = moments when Europa has a full moon  
- $C$ = moments when Ganymede has a full moon

Let $n_1$, $n_2$, $n_3$ = moments with exactly 1, 2, or 3 full moons respectively.

Given constraints:
- $n_1 + n_2 + n_3 = 54$ (total moments with at least one full moon)
- $n_2 + n_3 = 11$ (moments with at least two full moons)

**Verification:** The constraints make sense—we expect more moments with one moon than with multiple moons. ✓

---

## Step 2: Determine the number of moments with exactly one full moon

**What we're calculating:** Find $n_1$ from the system of equations.

**Calculation:**
From $n_1 + n_2 + n_3 = 54$ and $n_2 + n_3 = 11$:

$$n_1 = 54 - 11 = 43$$

**Verification:** 43 + 11 = 54 ✓

---

## Step 3: Express pairwise intersections in terms of orbital periods

**What we're calculating:** Relate set intersections to gcd of orbital periods $I$, $E$, $G$.

**Calculation:**
In a complete cycle of $\text{lcm}(I, E, G)$:
- $|A \cap B| = \gcd(I, E)$ (moments when both Io and Europa full)
- $|A \cap C| = \gcd(I, G)$ (moments when both Io and Ganymede full)
- $|B \cap C| = \gcd(E, G)$ (moments when both Europa and Ganymede full)
- $|A \cap B \cap C| = \gcd(I, E, G)$ (moments when all three full)

**Verification:** These are standard results using the relationship $\text{lcm}(a,b) \cdot \gcd(a,b) = a \cdot b$. ✓

---

## Step 4: Set up the key equation

**What we're calculating:** Express the constraint on multiple full moons using gcd values.

**Calculation:**
The number of moments with at least 2 full moons equals:
$$n_2 + n_3 = |A \cap B| + |A \cap C| + |B \cap C| - 2|A \cap B \cap C| = 11$$

Substituting gcd expressions:
$$\gcd(I, E) + \gcd(I, G) + \gcd(E, G) - 2\gcd(I, E, G) = 11$$

**Verification:** This formula correctly counts moments in at least 2 sets (avoiding double-counting of triple intersections). ✓

---

## Step 5: Find valid integer solutions

**What we're calculating:** Determine orbital periods $I$, $E$, $G$ satisfying the equation.

**Calculation:**
Let $d = \gcd(I, E, G)$ and write $I = di$, $E = de$, $G = dg$ where $\gcd(i, e, g) = 1$.

Then:
$$d[\gcd(i, e) + \gcd(i, g) + \gcd(e, g) - 2] = 11$$

Since 11 is prime, either:
- **Case 1:** $d = 1$ and $\gcd(i, e) + \gcd(i, g) + \gcd(e, g) = 13$
- **Case 2:** $d = 11$ and $\gcd(i, e) + \gcd(i, g) + \gcd(e, g) = 3$

For **Case 2**, if $i, e, g$ are pairwise coprime with $\gcd(i,e,g) = 1$:
- Try $i = 1, e = 1, g = 2$: $\gcd(1,1) + \gcd(1,2) + \gcd(1,2) = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3$ ✓

This gives: $I = 11$, $E = 11$, $G = 22$ (or permutations)

**Verification of solution:**
- $\gcd(11, 11) = 11$
- $\gcd(11, 22) = 11$  
- $\gcd(11, 22) = 11$
- $\gcd(11, 11, 22) = 11$

$$11 + 11 + 11 - 2(11) = 33 - 22 = 11$$ ✓

---

## Step 6: Calculate the final answer

**What we're calculating:** Find $I \cdot E \cdot G$.

**Calculation:**
$$I \cdot E \cdot G = 11 \cdot 11 \cdot 22 = 121 \cdot 22 = 2662$$

**Verification by substitution:** 
With these periods, the gcd conditions yield exactly 11 moments with multiple full moons, consistent with our constraint that $n_2 + n_3 = 11$. The periods are reasonable orbital periods (in some units), and the product is a reasonable integer. ✓

---

**FINAL ANSWER: 2662**
