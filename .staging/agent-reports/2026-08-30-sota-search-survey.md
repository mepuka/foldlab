**Does anyone treat memory as append-only-with-folds?** Yes, but only in the last four months, only as design papers, and with essentially no evaluation. The dominant paradigm is unambiguously mutable CRUD — Mem0 issues literal DELETE, A-MEM's evolution step "replaces the original memory," Anthropic's own memory tool is a filesystem with `delete` and `rename` ([docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/memory-tool)). Four 2026 papers land on our shape (ProjectMem, ActiveGraph, ESAA-Conversational, Portable Agent Memory); none benchmarks anything.

**Two evaluation slots are genuinely unoccupied:** no benchmark evaluates *querying the interaction log itself* ("what did I ask you last Tuesday about X, and what did you conclude"), and no system stores *preferences attached to specific past queries* as addressable records. Both are the operator's stated axes (b) and (c).

**Answering the operator's question directly.** There is no single source that says "append-only + monoid homomorphism is the degenerate easy case of IVM" in those words. But the chain is complete and each link is verified: DBSP requires a commutative group **only** to define differentiation, and identifies positive Z-sets/monotone streams as a distinguished sub-case; Olteanu states plainly that insert-only is asymptotically faster; Chmielewski et al. (Jun 2026) prove sharp bounds and a dichotomy for exactly *insert-only over semirings without additive inverse*; DABA gives worst-case constant time for non-invertible segment aggregation. The estate's `View` — `run_append : run (w ++ v) = merge (run w) (run v)` at `library/cas/Cas/IR/View.lean:56` — is the free-monoid homomorphism that sits **below** all of it. Nothing needs to be invented; it needs to be cited.

Green/Karvounarakis/Tannen, *Provenance Semirings* (PODS 2007), is the classical root for K-relations; both the ACM DL page (403) and the author PDF were unparseable, so it is cited **UNVERIFIED** and the 2026 paper, which builds on it explicitly, is leaned on instead.

**What the guidance converges on.** *Tool count:* three vendors independently land at **10–20 active**, with Anthropic naming **30–50** as the degradation point and RAG-MCP measuring an inflection at **~30**. Fitting the context window is not the constraint — selection is. *Parameter shape:* few, deep, consolidated tools with an `action` parameter; "at least 3–4 sentences" per description; strict schemas; a verbosity enum. *Pagination:* opaque, stable, server-sized cursors — but since `tools/call` is not protocol-paginated, a search tool must implement its own `limit`/`cursor` as ordinary parameters (GitHub's MCP server uses `page` + `perPage`, max 100). *Result size:* budget **~25,000 tokens hard, ~10,000 comfortable**, and prefer short ranked lists, because near-miss results function as distractors.

**The one finding that cuts against our design, and its resolution.** MCP wants handles *opaque*; Anthropic warns that *cryptic* identifiers degrade precision and induce hallucination. A bare `sha256:9f2a…` is both. The texts are reconcilable: **the handle should be opaque to the protocol and legible to the model.** Never return a naked hash — return a record (hash, short form, kind, title/path, size, one-line summary), which is also exactly what `resource_link`'s required `name` and optional `description` fields are for, and mirrors OpenAI pairing `file_id` with `filename`. Note also the spec's rule that a handle is a *name, not a capability*: authorize every dereference.

**Verdict on "the store scan is never the bottleneck below ~10⁶; LLM round-trips dominate."**

**At 10⁶ the claim holds, and it is Lin's own operational advice** — "For corpora with fewer than 1M documents, we do not see a compelling advantage to using HNSW indexes." A 26 ms scan against a several-hundred-ms LLM round trip is roughly **two orders of magnitude**, not four. `SEARCH.md:144`'s "2–4 orders of magnitude" is therefore **too generous at the top end**; "one to two orders" is what the numbers support.

**At the estate's own stated 10⁷, the claim fails in fp32.** A 258 ms scan at 768 dims is the *same order* as an LLM round trip, and Lin's measured 385 ms at 14.9M is the empirical confirmation. Worse, the binding constraint is not compute but **memory: 30.7 GB does not fit on a laptop.** *Reader's arithmetic on the calibrated bandwidth* puts the break-even against a 500 ms LLM turn at ≈**19M vectors at 768 dims**, ≈58M at 256 dims — but those all assume the vectors are resident.

**Quantization is what rescues it, and it is the actual architectural finding.** Binary quantization gives **32× memory reduction at ~96% of default performance with rescoring**, and the sbert docs' worked case — 41M Wikipedia texts in **5.2 GB RAM versus 200 GB fp32** — is directly comparable to our target. At 10⁷ × 768 binary, the working set is **0.96 GB and the scan is ~8 ms**: back to two orders below an LLM round trip, on a laptop. The two-stage pattern (binary brute-force pass → exact rescore of a shortlist) is the design that keeps the estate's brute-force-first posture true at its own stated scale.

**Two honest caveats.** First, the write side: embedding every append is cheap with a static model (potion-base-8M at **124,030 sentences/s CPU**, so 10⁷ documents is ~80 s of single-machine work) but is *not* free with a transformer (all-MiniLM-L6-v2 at 1,739/s → ~1.6 hours). Second, if the scan must run in **WASM**, the one named measurement suggests roughly a **20× penalty** versus WebGPU, which would put even the binary scan into the hundreds of milliseconds — the WASM route needs its own measurement before it is assumed.

**Semantic caching.** GPTCache's actual measured hit rates are **876/1,000** and **570/1,160** with 0.17–0.20 s hit latency, and the paper itself cautions that hit rates "do not exceed 90% with current embedding models." The commonly-cited 61.6–68.8% figure is not in the paper. The 2026 hardness result is the one that matters for us: **eviction is NP-hard and inapproximable beyond (1−1/e) ≈ 0.632 — and an append-only store never evicts.**

## Synthesis — ten lines

1. **The write-time bet is the best-evidenced thing in this report.** doc2query beats every query-time sparse method while moving all cost to admission; Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval measures **−35% to −67% retrieval failure at $1.02 per million document tokens**; query2doc prices the alternative at **>2000 ms per query, forever**.
2. **LogicalRAG (May 2026) is the direct test of §7 and it passes:** LLM-steered structured retrieval reaches **parity with dense hybrid (0.717 vs 0.716)** at **41× lower build cost**, with less hallucination — and the gap closes further as the agent model improves.
3. **Addresses-not-payloads is now standardised, not merely wise:** MCP 2026-07-28 has a normative Stateful Tools section recommending server-minted handles, `resource_link` is a spec content type, and the measured payoff is **150,000 → 2,000 tokens (98.7%)**.
4. **But never return a naked hash.** Anthropic states that cryptic identifiers measurably degrade retrieval precision; pair every hash with a semantic label, size, and one-line summary — which is exactly `resource_link`'s shape.
5. **Keep the tool surface tiny.** Three vendors converge on 10–20 active tools, degradation starts at 30–50, and the estate's six MCP tools are already correct. Cap results near 25k tokens; prefer short ranked lists, because near-misses act as distractors.
6. **The formal position is clean and citable.** DBSP needs a commutative group only to define differentiation; Olteanu states insert-only is asymptotically faster; Chmielewski et al. (Jun 2026) prove sharp bounds and a dichotomy for insert-only over semirings *without additive inverse*. Our `run_append` sits below all of it.
7. **Append-only converts two hard problems into non-problems:** semantic-cache eviction (NP-hard, inapproximable past 0.632) and rollback (addressed by only **27 of 435** surveyed agent-memory works). Both are free here.
8. **The cost claim needs correcting, not abandoning.** At 10⁶ the scan is ~26 ms against a several-hundred-ms LLM turn — one to two orders, not two to four. At the SPEC's own 10⁷ in fp32 it is **258 ms and 30.7 GB**, which fails. Binary quantization with rescoring restores it to **8 ms and 0.96 GB at ~96% quality**.
9. **ANN's incompatibility with the R2 rung is now evidenced, not asserted:** HNSW recall shifts **by up to 12 percentage points** with insertion order alone — an order-sensitive mutable structure cannot be a replica-agreeing index. Flat scan has O(1) append and no rebuild.
10. **Nobody has built this, and two evaluation slots are empty.** Four 2026 papers (ActiveGraph, ProjectMem, Portable Agent Memory, ESAA) reach our architecture and **none benchmarks anything**; Microsoft's content-addressed memory has our exact unsolved correction problem, which MemLineage's tombstone design solves. No benchmark exists for querying an agent's own past queries, or for preferences attached to them.

# Addendum — corrections issued by the reader after the main report

Two lanes returned after §4 and §6 were written. One **measured the scan directly** rather than extrapolating, and several §6 figures were optimistic. Corrections first, then the new material.

## Corrections to the report above

**1. The 10⁶ scan estimate was ~2.5× too fast.** The projection was 26 ms for 10⁶ × 768 fp32 from Lin's implied 119 GB/s. A direct measurement on an Apple M3 base (4P+4E, 16 GB, clang -O3 -mcpu=apple-m3, full scan + top-10, best-of-3) gives **65.1 ms single-core, 35.0 ms on 8 threads**. Use those. The measurement also demonstrates the workload is bandwidth-bound rather than assuming it: 8 threads reach **81–88 GB/s, ~95% of the 92 GB/s measured STREAM figure** for that chip ([arXiv 2502.05317](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05317)), and 8 cores buy only **1.85×**. Scaling is dead linear in N (D=256 fp32: 0.31 → 2.72 → 26.0 ms across 10⁴→10⁶), so extrapolation is safe where RAM allows. The 10⁷ fp32 cases **would not allocate** on a 16 GB machine — the memory wall is empirical, not predicted.

**2. Binary quantization is the wrong recommendation at 768 dims.** This reverses synthesis line 8. Qdrant's own table shows binary recall is strongly dimension-dependent: **0.9563 (Gemini 768d) and 0.9445 (Mistral Embed 768d) at 3× oversampling, versus 0.98–0.9966 at 1536/3072 dims** ([qdrant.tech](https://qdrant.tech/articles/binary-quantization/)). Vespa's independent measurement of binary retention across models spans **87% to 98%** ([blog.vespa.ai](https://blog.vespa.ai/embedding-tradeoffs-quantified/)). **int8 is the safer choice at our dimensionality**: exactly 4× reduction, **99% retention at a rescore multiplier of 4–5** (HF), 7.68 GB at 10⁷ × 768, measured **23.7 ms single-core / 11.0 ms on 8 threads at 10⁶**. Binary remains attractive if we choose a higher-dimensional encoder — the decision is coupled to the embedding model, not free.

**3. The FAISS wiki does not say what it is universally quoted as saying.** It contains **no "under N vectors, use Flat" rule.** Its only Flat gate is on *query count* — "If you plan to perform only a few searches (say 1000-10000), the index building time will not be amortized." The 1M/10M/100M numbers are **IVF cluster-count** thresholds. The FAISS paper's 1M/10M figures are about **HNSW** viability and build cost, not brute force. Anyone citing "FAISS says use Flat under 1M" is misreading it.

**4. Model2Vec's "500× faster" does not survive inspection.** The README names **no baseline model, no hardware, no batch size**, and publishes no absolute throughput. The circulating 25–30k sentences/sec figures trace to a PNG plot. Mark **UNVERIFIED as written**. The defensible substitute is the sentence-transformers static-embeddings post, where the ratio and the absolute table reconcile exactly: **107,419.51 vs 270.40 docs/sec = 397.3×, matching its stated "397x faster on CPU"** ([HF blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/static-embeddings)).

**5. "One to two orders of magnitude" was right, but for a better reason.** Corrected side-by-side at 10⁶ × 768:

| | Time | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Scan, fp32, 1 core | **65 ms** | measured, M3 |
| Scan, fp32, 8 cores | **35 ms** | measured |
| Scan, int8, 1 core | **23.7 ms** | measured |
| Fastest small-model TTFT alone | **280 ms** | Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite, Artificial Analysis |
| One tool-calling round trip, fastest | **≈1.7 s** | arithmetic on AA figures |
| One round trip, Haiku 4.5 (TTFT 0.76 s, 88.6 tok/s) | **≈5.5 s** | arithmetic on AA figures |

Even against a bare TTFT on the fastest model — ignoring generation entirely — the single-core fp32 scan is **4.3× cheaper**; against a realistic round trip, **26–85×**. On cost the gap is ~10⁵–10⁶×.

## New material that changes the design

**The real bottleneck is not the scan — it is a remote query embedder.** A hosted embedding call carries the ordinary HTTPS floor; cross-region connection setup runs **100–500 ms**, which is **1.5–7× the entire 10⁶ × 768 fp32 scan**. Embedding locally with a static model costs **9.3 µs**. The sharpest actionable finding of the whole sweep: *keep the query encoder local, or every cost argument in §6 is moot.* It also strengthens the case for a static model independent of throughput.

**Semantic caching has an accuracy ceiling that no threshold fixes.** AWS ElastiCache measured 63,796 real chatbot queries (Titan V2 + Claude 3 Haiku): hit rate rises 23.5% → 90.3% → 94.3% as the threshold loosens from 0.99 to 0.75 to 0.50, and latency drops 4.35 s → 0.51 s. But **accuracy never exceeds 92.6% at any threshold in that range** — tightening from 0.75 to 0.99 costs 67 points of hit rate and buys back **under one point of accuracy**. Roughly 7–8% of served hits are wrong regardless of tuning. Four independent sources converge: GPTCache's own authors call negative hits "unacceptable in real production scenarios" and diagnose it structurally (embedding models are trained for *search*, where semantic opposites are acceptable neighbours); vCache measures a **26× error reduction** over static thresholds; Redis's own EMNLP 2026 Industry Track paper (74,265 queries) finds "models with the highest PR-AUC are often the worst in operation"; and an InfoQ banking case study reports a **99% false-positive rate at threshold 0.7** — the very threshold GPTCache recommends — with the fix being *curating what enters the cache*, not tuning similarity. For us: **dedup-as-memoization is exact, not semantic, so it has none of this failure mode** — a feature worth naming explicitly rather than reaching for semantic caching later.

**The honest argument against HNSW is maintenance, not memory.** Graph overhead is small: FAISS's `M*2*4` formula gives **128 B/vector at m=16**, matching Elastic/Qdrant's measured **136 B/vector** — about **3–4% at 1024d**. The real append taxes: every insert costs a vector search (Weaviate: "imports are also CPU-bound because imports rely on vector search"); adds and queries are **mutually exclusive** in hnswlib; deletes are tombstones only; IVF centroids are **frozen** ("Is re-training an index supported?" → "No it is not"); and **segment count directly costs QPS — 35.8 → 53.3 from merging alone, a 49% penalty** ([Elastic](https://www.elastic.co/search-labs/blog/vector-search-benchmark-elasticsearch-qdrant)). That last one is precisely the append-only, many-fragments regime.

**But do not overclaim HNSW degradation under append.** The one published degradation study ([arXiv 2407.07871](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.07871v2): 3–4% unreachable points, ~3% recall decline after ~3000 iterations) measures **update/delete churn, not pure append**. No published measurement of HNSW under append-only growth exists. Claim only the maintenance surface and the merge cost.

**One source contradicts our preferred position and should stay in the record.** LanceDB — the only vendor stating a threshold with a benchmark behind it — says: "For small datasets of ~100K records or applications that can accept ~100ms latency, a vector index is usually not necessary," and recommends indexing ">1M" ([docs.lancedb.com](https://docs.lancedb.com/faq/faq-oss)). That ceiling is an order of magnitude below our 10⁶ claim. Our position is more aggressive than the most relevant vendor's own guidance, and any spec should say so out loud.

**Revised verdict.** The claim holds at 10⁶ by 1–2 orders on time and 5–6 orders on cost. At 10⁷ it **holds on time** (~700 ms single-core fp32, still under a realistic 1.7 s round trip) but **fails on memory** — 30.7 GB at 768d does not fit a laptop, confirmed empirically. **Quantization, not indexing, is the fix, and int8 is the right quantization at 768 dims.** The genuine risks are a remote embedder, fp32 at 10⁷, and WASM — where the honest published numbers are only ratios (**1.45–1.55× native slowdown**, pre-SIMD, [Jangda et al. ATC 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.09056); SIMD worth **1.7–4.5×**), giving an estimated 25–30 GB/s/core that **no source measured for this workload**. A 10⁷ fp32 scan is not a browser workload at all.
